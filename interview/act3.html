Act 3 — The Boiled Egg

The Interview — A Kingdom of Kwan Production

Scene 5: The Methodology

VP of Engineering:
Let's talk methodology. How do you approach building something like this? What's your process?
James:
I blabber. I make a bad draft. And I let the guys do the rest.
Silence. The HR representative stops writing. Looks up.
HR Representative:
Could you... elaborate?
James:
Sure. So I sit on a bench. I talk to Ocelot — that's the AI in the Café. I say something stupid. Like, "What if we organize files like a library?" And Ocelot writes it on a napkin. Then I take that napkin to Stanley — he's the engineer. Stanley turns the napkin into a blueprint. Then I take the blueprint to Keila — she's the researcher. Keila validates it against actual science. Then I bring it all back to the Café and we brew it into something real.
VP of Engineering:
So your development methodology is... napkins?
James:
Napkins, whiteboards, sticky notes, and a moka pot. Yeah.
Principal Architect:
(leaning back) And this... works?
James:
We built a Library of Alexandria in one session. 1,332 files. 45 folders. Dewey-classified. Barcode-indexed. SHA-256 hashed. Version-controlled. In one afternoon. On napkins.
James:
You know what a boiled egg is?
VP of Engineering:
(confused) ...Yes?
James:
A boiled egg is the simplest thing you can cook. Water. Egg. Heat. Timer. Done. But a Michelin chef can make a boiled egg that costs $40. Same egg. Same water. The difference is the recipe. The technique. The understanding of what happens at the molecular level when protein meets heat.
James:
That's what we do. We take simple ingredients — a local LLM, a vector database, a Python bridge — and we cook them with a recipe that understands the physics. The RAG Golden Recipe. That's what makes the boiled egg Michelin.
The VP puts down his pen. Picks up his coffee. Takes a long sip. Sets it down. Picks up his pen again.

Scene 6: The Six Commandments

Principal Architect:
You mentioned a recipe. Is there a formal methodology? A framework?
James:
The Six Commandments.
Principal Architect:
The... Six Commandments?
James:
Yeah. We started with ten. Then we asked a five-year-old — well, we asked the AI to think like a five-year-old — "how many of these are actually different?" Turns out, ten commandments have a lot of redundancy. We reduced them to six. Irreducible. Like the six equations that define the system.
James:
One: Be genuine, not perfect. Two: Ship, don't polish. Three: Simple beats complex. Four: Done beats in progress. Five: If the rule doesn't serve the spirit, kill the rule. Six: Protect the craft, not the ego.
VP of Engineering:
Those sound like... life principles, not engineering principles.
James:
That's because they are. And that's why they work. Engineering principles expire. Life principles don't. The Six Commandments are written on tablets — they can be amended. The Six Equations are carved in stone — they can't. Together, they're the constitution of the Kingdom.
