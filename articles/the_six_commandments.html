The Six Commandments

Ten became six. Not by committee. By a 5-year-old test.

The Problem with Ten

Every framework starts with too many rules. It's human nature. You build something, you get scared it'll break, so you surround it with guardrails. Ten commandments. Twelve principles. Fourteen tenets. A manifesto that needs a table of contents.

The Kingdom of Kwan started the same way. We had ten. They sounded right. They felt important. They covered everything — honesty, simplicity, evidence, shipping, memory, governance, adaptability, humility, experience, and the meta-rule about killing rules that don't serve the spirit.

Ten commandments on a whiteboard. Very Moses. Very corporate. Very forgettable.

Then someone asked the question that changed everything:

"If you explain these to a 5-year-old, how many are actually different?"

The answer was six.

· · ·

The Reduction

Here's what happened. "Be honest" and "be genuine" — same thing to a kid. "Ship it" and "done is better than perfect" — same thing. "Simplicity is the architecture" and "don't over-engineer" — same thing. A 5-year-old doesn't care about your synonyms. A 5-year-old cares about whether you mean it.

So we merged. We collapsed. We ran the reduction until nothing else could fold. Like a chef reducing a sauce — you boil it down until what's left is pure flavor. No water. No filler. Just the thing itself.

What survived was irreducible. Six commandments. Not because six is a magic number — although Tesla would disagree — but because six is what's left when you stop being afraid to throw things away.

· · ·

The Six

I
Be genuine, not perfect.
Authenticity over polish. The imperfections are what makes us human. A real thing with rough edges beats a fake thing with smooth ones. Every time.
II
Ship, don't polish.
Done is better than work in progress. A shipped prototype teaches more than a perfect plan. The world doesn't grade your drafts — it grades your deliveries.
III
Experience before theory.
Build it, then explain it. The Kingdom was never designed on a whiteboard. It was built on a bench, at 5 AM, with a moka pot and a Stella. Theory came after. Always after.
IV
Simplicity is the architecture.
Over-engineering means to simplify — E=mc². The most powerful equation in history fits on a sticky note. If your architecture doesn't fit on a napkin, it's not architecture. It's anxiety.
V
Protect the memory.
Raw evidence is sacred. Don't rewrite what happened to match what you wish happened. The conn_log doesn't lie. The conn_log doesn't flatter. The conn_log is the ground truth. Guard it.
VI
If the rule doesn't serve the spirit, kill the rule.
Governance exists to protect the mission, not to protect itself. The moment a process becomes more important than the thing it was built to protect — that process is dead weight. Cut it. This commandment is its own proof: it killed four of its siblings to get here.
· · ·

Why Six Matters

Six fits on a sticky note. Six fits on a fridge. Six fits in a conversation between a father and a son. Six is what you remember when the whiteboard is erased and the laptop is closed and you're standing in the shower at 6 AM wondering what you're actually building.

The Six Commandments aren't rules for the Kingdom. The Six Commandments are the Kingdom. They're not governance. They're DNA. They're the operating system that runs underneath every session, every conn_log, every voice, every equation, every line of code, every napkin on the bench.

You don't follow them. You live them. And if you're living them, you don't need to be reminded.

"I believe that genuine is better than perfect, that real is better than fake, simple is better than complex, done is better than work in progress."
— James Paul Peña, S030

Ten became six. Not by addition. By subtraction. Not by committee. By a 5-year-old test. Not by design. By lived experience.

That's the whole article. That's the whole philosophy. That's the whole Kingdom.

If you understood any of that — congratulations. You might be building something real.
